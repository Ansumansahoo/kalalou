The Kalalou Story

Since 1986 — from a Jamaican vacation to nine countries, 1,500 products, and 100 Mississippi employees.

It began in 1984 in the market square of Ocho Rios, Jamaica. Susan Williams picked up a hand-woven basket and told her husband Doug she'd been paying $40 for one like it back home in Mississippi. The Jamaican price: two dollars.

Doug and Susan were doing flea markets in those days, painting on old barn wood to make ends meet. On the plane home their friends helped them carry armfuls of baskets. Back in Jackson, Doug couldn't get the 2,000% markup out of his head. He talked the Jamaican Trade Council into shipping a container of baskets with no money down and 30 days to pay. That was miracle one of many.

Two years of flea market weekends later, a shopper suggested the Dallas Wholesale Show. In July 1986, Doug and Susan brought their first watermelon basket — Susan's idea, inspired by the round shape of the Jamaican baskets — and Country Originals was born. Two years running (1991, 1992) they made Inc. Magazine's 500 Fastest Growing Privately Held Companies.

Today

Today the company is called Kalalou — Creole for a soup that's never made the same way twice, made in a social environment of family and friends. That name captures the ever-changing mix of artisan products the company brings back from the lesser-known corners of the world.

Kalalou is still owned by its founders, Doug and Susan Williams. It operates from a 150,000-square-foot distribution center in Jackson, Mississippi, employs 100 people, and works with artisans in nine countries.

Giving Back

Over the years the company has developed charitable projects in several of the countries where it does business — Doug & Susan's Kids Foundation covers medical care, education, food and shelter for children in Haiti, Colombia, Honduras, the Philippines, and Mississippi. All administrative costs are absorbed by Kalalou so 100% of donations reach the kids.

Fun fact The best friends who helped carry those first baskets home from Jamaica — Andy and Debbie — are still best friends with Doug and Susan today. They're neighbors on a Mississippi lake with an island they call KiWi Island (Kilpatrick + Williams).

Haiti · Sister Clara's Clinic

When our Haitian manufacturer built the medical center for Sister Clara, none of us knew what an impact it would have on the local community in Port-au-Prince. Sister Clara runs the clinic with the efficiency of a staff sergeant. It's free for those who cannot pay and accepts whatever donations from those who can. Wednesday is children's day.

Kalalou donates all proceeds from the sale of Sister Clara's cross directly to the clinic — no government agency, no charitable organization in between. Every penny goes to her, and she uses it for medical supplies and to pay two doctors.

Honduras · The Atuto Project

"Atuto" is Spanish for "piggy back." The project was set up to help children in the tiny village where our Honduran products are made — many without parents, without regular meals, without school. Four organized kitchens were set up in local homes.

Today the kids are healthy and attending school regularly. Kalalou helped build a daycare and after-school center. On a recent visit, our Honduran manufacturer told us about the amazement in the children's eyes when school uniforms, paper, and pencils were handed out.

Ellipsis International (Philippines)

This project helps street kids who are homeless and endangered. Children are rescued from the streets and given a home, food, education, and — most importantly — love and attention. Kalalou funds it through the sale of an oyster cross made in the Philippines. Learn more at ellipsisinternational.org.

Colombia · Tom Sawyer House

"Tom Sawyer" is what Doug and Susan called a nine-year-old boy they met selling fossils on the side of a Colombian road — light hair, green eyes, freckles, and a floppy hat that gave him the name. He wasn't an orphan; he lived with seven siblings, a grandfather, and a mother he loved, in a two-room mud-floor house with no plumbing.

Kalalou put his mother — an expert crocheter — to work making purses. Every bag they buy supports a tiny business and gives a mother of eight the means to feed her family. The kids are now in school, dressed in new clothes, and eating well. In 2005 they moved into a brand-new three-bedroom house with running water.

Update With the help of Doug & Susan's Kids Foundation, Andrés Filipé (Tom Sawyer) graduated university and now works for a logistics company in Bogotá.

The Unintended Casualties of the Tariff War

Small American businesses like Kalalou face a wave of tariffs — but there's a simple fix that turns the problem into a win for employees, government, and the economy.

The problem

When politicians talk about tariffs, they frame them as tools to protect American manufacturing. But there's a group of overlooked victims — small-to-medium American companies that can't make their products in the U.S. and yet employ millions of Americans.

Kalalou is one of them. A true "American Dream" story — born in a Mississippi garage 40 years ago, now supplying 10,000 retail stores, employing 100 hardworking Mississippians, and operating from a 150,000-square-foot distribution center. Doug and Susan Williams have spent decades forging relationships with artisans in seven countries to source unique, handcrafted items. None of these products can be made in the U.S.

In 2024 Kalalou paid substantial tariffs. In 2025 that burden doubled. If the trend continues, Kalalou will pay up to $1M in tariffs by year end — a crushing blow to a company of its size, and to 100 Mississippi families.

The proposal: the Employee Profit Share Tax Credit

Susan Williams is not one to sit back and wait. Her proposal turns the tariff problem into a win-win:

  • Companies that import goods and share 10% of net profits with their employees qualify.
  • Qualifying companies receive 90% of their tariff payments back from the government.
  • The government keeps 10% — tariffs stay in place as a negotiation tool.

Everyone wins

  • Government wins — tariffs remain in place as a powerful geopolitical tool.
  • Employees win — a substantial profit-sharing check at year end.
  • Economy wins — that extra income gets spent, fueling local economies and small businesses.
  • Small businesses win — companies like Kalalou stay in business and keep American jobs.
Bottom line This isn't a bailout. It's a blueprint for economic growth that rewards companies for sharing success with their American workforce — and it costs the government $0. If Washington is serious about protecting American jobs, it's time to look at who's actually carrying the weight of these tariffs.
